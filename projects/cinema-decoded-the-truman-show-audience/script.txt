Millions of people know Truman is being watched. But almost nobody tries to stop it.

Every neighbour, conversation, and coincidence in Seahaven is staged by a billion-dollar television broadcast. Yet Truman genuinely believes his life is ordinary.

Outside the dome, millions of viewers watch his deepest heartbreaks, private anxieties, and daily routines. They feel real affection for him—and they never turn the television off.

That is the film's chilling contradiction: their entertainment requires his imprisonment. They can weep for his pain while silently demanding that the cameras keep rolling.

Truman thinks the entire world is watching him. But he doesn't realize that the people watching have become part of the reason he cannot escape.

And that's what makes the film so uncomfortable. Sometimes, caring about someone's story isn't the same as caring about their freedom.
